Reading Passage 3 — Cambridge 11 Test 1

Reducing the Effects of Climate Change

Mark Rowe reports on a pioneering scheme to help coastal communities adapt to rising sea levels.

A

According to the United Nations, climate change could displace hundreds of millions of people by the end of the century. Rising sea levels, combined with storm surges, threaten to inundate coastal areas where a quarter of the world's population lives. In Bangladesh, for example, a one-metre rise in sea level would flood about 17 per cent of the country, directly affecting about 15 million people. But it is not just rising waters that are the problem; salt water intrusion into fresh water supplies could render vast areas unfit for agriculture and human consumption.

B

Rather than attempting to stop the seas from rising, which most scientists agree is now inevitable, a growing number of experts are advocating a different approach: helping people adapt to the changing environment. This concept, known as 'adaptation', is gaining acceptance as a necessary strategy alongside efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions. One organisation at the forefront of this work is the Mangrove Restoration Project, operating in the Ganges-Brahmaputra delta in Bangladesh.

C

The project is based on a simple but effective principle: mangrove forests act as a natural barrier against the sea. Their complex root systems stabilise coastal land and reduce the force of waves, while the trees themselves absorb carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. Studies have shown that a 100-metre-wide belt of mangroves can reduce wave height by up to 50 per cent, significantly lowering the risk of flooding and erosion. Furthermore, mangroves provide a rich habitat for fish and other marine life, supporting local fishing communities and enhancing biodiversity.

D

Initially, local villagers were sceptical. They saw mangroves as competing for land that could be used for farming, and they were concerned that the trees would harbour snakes and other pests. The project team worked hard to address these concerns, demonstrating through pilot schemes that mangroves could be planted along boundaries and waterways without significantly reducing agricultural land. They also introduced species that were less attractive to snakes, and provided training in sustainable management practices.

E

The results have been remarkable. Over five years, more than 5,000 hectares of mangrove forest have been restored or planted. Coastal erosion has slowed by up to 40 per cent in some areas, and during severe storms, the damage to homes and farmland has been substantially reduced. Fish populations have increased, providing an additional source of food and income. Perhaps most importantly, the scheme has strengthened community cohesion, as villagers have worked together to plant and maintain the forests.

F

However, the project faces significant challenges. Rising sea levels may outpace the ability of mangroves to grow upwards, especially if the land is also sinking due to subsidence. Pollution from agricultural runoff and industrial waste can also damage the delicate ecosystem, while over-harvesting of timber and fuelwood threatens to undo years of progress. Long-term funding remains uncertain, and the project's leaders acknowledge that continued international support will be essential.

G

Despite these concerns, the Mangrove Restoration Project has become a model for other coastal regions around the world. Similar schemes are now being implemented in Vietnam, India, and West Africa, using the lessons learned in Bangladesh. What began as a local initiative has grown into a global movement, proving that adaptation measures can be both effective and affordable — if they involve the communities they are designed to protect.
