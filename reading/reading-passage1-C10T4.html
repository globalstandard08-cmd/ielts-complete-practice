Reading Passage 1 — Land of the Rising Sun

Japan has a significantly better record in terms of average mathematical attainment than England and Wales. Large sample international comparisons of pupils' attainments since the 1960s have established that not only did Japanese pupils at age 13 have better scores of average attainment, but there was also a larger proportion of 'low' attainers in England, where, incidentally, the variation in attainment scores was much greater. The percentage of Gross National Product spent on education is reasonably similar in the two countries, so how is this higher and more consistent attainment in maths achieved?

There are two key explanations for this excellence in mathematics education in Japan: the nature of education and the way it is organised.

First, the type of education offered in Japan is based on the belief that all children can learn to high standards if they are given the right conditions. There is no streaming or setting in primary or lower secondary schools, and all pupils follow the same curriculum. Teachers are also expected to adapt their teaching to the needs of all pupils, and to spend time helping those who are struggling.

Second, the organisation of schooling in Japan also contributes to high attainment. The school year is longer than in England and Wales, starting in April and ending in March, with six weeks of summer holiday, two weeks in winter and two weeks in spring. Pupils also spend more time at school each day, and much of this time is spent on whole-class teaching. Teachers are well-qualified and highly respected, and they have opportunities to develop their skills through in-service training and observation of other teachers.

Another important factor is the value placed on education by Japanese society. Parents are very involved in their children's education, and many pay for extra tuition at juku, or cram schools, outside normal school hours. There is also a strong belief that effort and hard work are more important than natural ability in determining success. This attitude is reinforced by the way children are taught in school, where they are encouraged to compete against their own previous performance rather than against others.

But what is the secret of Japan's success in mathematics? Is it the way the subject is taught, or something else? Research suggests that the approach to teaching mathematics in Japan is very different from that in England. In Japan, the emphasis is on developing a deep understanding of mathematical concepts, rather than just memorising rules and procedures. Teachers spend more time introducing new ideas and helping pupils to see the connections between different areas of mathematics. They also encourage pupils to think for themselves and to explain their reasoning.

Lessons in Japan are typically structured in the same way. The teacher begins by presenting a problem or a situation that the pupils are asked to think about. They then work on the problem individually or in small groups, before sharing their ideas with the whole class. The teacher guides the discussion, helping pupils to clarify their thinking and to develop more efficient methods. Finally, the teacher summarises the key points and shows how the new idea can be applied in different contexts.

This approach requires a great deal of skill and preparation on the part of the teacher. Japanese teachers spend a lot of time working together to plan lessons and to develop teaching materials. They also observe each other's lessons and give feedback, which helps them to improve their practice.
